The view from the middle

Almost everyone thinks they're average.

Ask people across Britain where they sit on the income ladder and you'll hear the same answer over and over: somewhere in the middle.

The cleaner says it. The teacher says it. The management consultant on £120,000 says it too. But we can't all be in the middle — and the people most likely to misjudge their place are the ones standing nearest the top.

This is the affluence perception gap. It's measurable, it's surprisingly large, and it quietly shapes how the country votes, gives and governs. Let's start with what the ladder actually looks like.

The perception gap

Why does the top feel like the middle?

Because we don't compare ourselves to the country. We compare ourselves to the people just above us — the bigger house, the second holiday, the private school we didn't manage. Look only upward, and there is always someone richer to feel ordinary against.

Researchers have measured this directly. When you ask people which tenth of the population they belong to, the answers bend hard toward the middle — at both ends.

Self-perceived vs. actual position on the income ladder
1

If everyone knew their place…

…the picture would be a straight diagonal. People in the poorest tenth would say "poorest tenth"; people in the richest tenth would say "richest tenth". Reality and perception would match.

2

Here's what people actually say.

The real line is bent flat. Whatever their true position, people's guesses cluster around the middle of the scale. Hardly anyone places themselves at the extremes.

3

The richest barely feel above average.

Those in the top tenth, on average, put themselves only a little over halfway up.

"I feel fairly middle of the road and average — but objectively I know that's completely untrue."
— a top-10% earner, interviewed by researchers

4

The poorest do the opposite.

People near the bottom guess higher than they are. From every direction, the answer is the same: "I'm normal." The gap is just biggest, and most consequential, at the top.

Income is only half the story

Now do the same thing with wealth.

Income is what lands in your account each month. Wealth is what you already own — the house, the pension, the savings, the shares. And wealth in Britain is far, far more lopsided than income.

Wealthy but worried

So why doesn't any of it feel like wealth?

A six-figure income sounds like the finish line. In practice, a household on £100,000 sits in roughly the top 5% of the country — and still, in the words of one bank's own guide, gets caught "between being rich and feeling wealthy".

They keep six months of expenses in reserve — and still worry, mostly about the mortgage rate. To them, this is "just about managing". To the other 95% of the country, it is the dream.

This is the engine of the perception gap. We anchor to the people just ahead of us, never to the country behind us. And the feeling has a sting in the tail.

When researchers interviewed Britain's top 10% over several years, they found something uncomfortable: the more people had, the more insecure they felt — and the more firmly they opposed taxes and redistribution. Britain's wealthy turned out to be more anti-welfare than the wealthy of almost any comparable country. Convinced they were ordinary, they voted and gave as if they were.

A moving target

And the ground keeps shifting beneath everyone.

Affluence isn't fixed. A salary that felt generous in 2007 buys far less today. Shock after shock — a financial crash, a pandemic, an energy crisis, the next war — has quietly rewritten what your money is actually worth, and how rich you feel saying the number out loud.

The catch is that inflation hides the damage. Your payslip still shows the same figure, or even a bigger one, so it doesn't feel like getting poorer — even when it is. Economists call it the money illusion. Britain has lived through a brutal lesson in it.

Real take-home pay over time (2008 = 100)
1

Pay used to rise like clockwork.

For decades before 2008, the typical worker's real wages climbed by roughly 2% a year, almost without fail. Each generation could expect to be better off than the last.

2

Had that simply continued…

…the average worker would be around £11,000 a year better off today. This dashed line is the prosperity Britain was on track for.

3

The 2008 crash broke the trend.

Real wages flatlined for fifteen years — the worst pay stagnation since the Napoleonic Wars. The shaded wedge is growth that never arrived. Meanwhile cheap-money policy inflated house and share prices, quietly rewarding those who already owned assets.

4

Then the shocks came faster.

A pandemic, then an energy crisis supercharged by the war in Ukraine, drove inflation to 11.1% in 2022 — a 41-year high. Typical energy bills leapt from about £1,000 to £2,500. Pay shrank in real terms even as the numbers on the payslip grew.

5

The next one is never far away.

In June 2025 a twelve-day war between Israel and Iran sent oil spiking once more. It receded within weeks — but the lesson stuck: real affluence now lives one shock away from the headlines.

The cruel twist

Shocks make us feel equal — while pulling us apart.

When prices surge, everyone feels it, so everyone says the same thing: "we're all struggling." Even the genuinely well-off feel the pinch — which only hardens the top 10%'s conviction that they, too, are just about managing.

But "we're all in it together" is the illusion. The very same shocks that make affluence feel universal actually widen the real gap, because they land on a country split in two.

If you owned assets

A cushion that grew

During the pandemic, locked-down higher earners couldn't spend — the savings ratio hit a record 25.9% and households banked an estimated £125–180bn, overwhelmingly at the top. House prices and pensions, lifted by cheap money, pushed their paper wealth higher still.

If you relied on wages

A blow with no buffer

Lower earners were likelier to be furloughed or lose work, with little saved to fall back on. When inflation hit there was no asset cushion to soften it — just energy bills, rising rents and, for many, fresh debt or skipped meals.

That is the perception gap's nastiest feature. A crisis feels like shared sacrifice, breeding a sense that everyone is roughly in the same boat — at the exact moment the distance between the asset-rich top and the wage-dependent rest is growing fastest of all.

Why the gap matters

If the people with the most are sure they're ordinary, they'll govern as if they are.

The perception gap isn't a harmless quirk of modesty. It shapes who we think deserves help, whose taxes feel "fair", and whose struggles count as real. A country that can't see its own wealth can't have an honest argument about how to share it.

Closing the gap doesn't require agreeing on what to do. It just requires seeing the ladder clearly — including the part you're standing on.
